Bridging the Great Divide in Climate Adaptation:
Top-Down Adaptive Planning meets Human-Centred Design

Climate change adaptation for vulnerable urban neighbourhoods remains one of the most pressing challenges of our time. While adaptation studies recognize the intertwined complexities of built environments and societal vulnerabilities, methodological frameworks for harnessing the agency of marginalized communities remain generic.

Academic Research Mandate • Core Aim & 4 Strategic Operational Goals
Target Cities: Matched Secondary Coastal Cities (~115k–310k pop)
🎯 Overarching Research Aim Bridging Top-Down & Bottom-Up Approaches

To bridge the fundamental methodological divide between top-down, expert-driven adaptive planning and bottom-up human-centred design by establishing an evidence-based, multi-scalar spatial and visual methodology that operationalizes the agency of marginalized communities in statutory climate adaptation decisions across matching secondary coastal cities in the Global South and Global North.

The Core Empirical Problem: Adaptation studies recognize the intertwined complexities of built environments and societal vulnerabilities, yet methodological frameworks for harnessing the interests of marginalized communities remain generic. Top-down planning models treat communities as passive recipients, risking maladaptation and livelihood severance (South) or unembanked exclusion and climate gentrification (North). Conversely, human-centred design captures rich vernacular survival knowledge, but spatial planning authorities fail to integrate these insights into statutory zoning or capital budgets.

Goal 1 • Empirical Scale 1:50,000

Socio-Spatial Disconnect Audit

Quantify and spatially audit the disconnect between macro-engineering compliance and localized lived vulnerability, diagnosing how top-down seawalls cause livelihood severance (South) or unembanked social housing exclusion (North).

Instrument: Arup CRI (156 indicators) & InSAR
Deliverable: Baseline Disconnect Index

Paradigm I • Top-Down Expert-Driven / Technocratic

Adaptive Planning

Operates through macro hydraulic modeling, centralized capital infrastructure contracts, and rigid zoning bylaws. Focuses on system preservation and engineering resistance to 1:50 or 1:100-year storm surge events.

Agency Role: Communities are treated as passive recipients or subjected to the "illusion of inclusion"—consultative public hearings without substantive decision-making authority (Few, Brown, & Tompkins, 2007; Arnstein, 1969).
Spatial Blind Spot: Treats vulnerable settlements as uniform, homogeneous hazard polygons; entirely blind to customary boat moorings, informal tenure, and women's fish-drying commons.
Failure Mode: Monolithic seawalls sever fisherfolk from the sea and trap internal pluvial runoff (South), or defensive dikes accelerate climate gentrification and displace unembanked social housing (North) (Anguelovski et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2016).
Literature: Few et al. (2007) • Anguelovski et al. (2016) • Pelling (2011)

Paradigm II • Bottom-Up Community-Oriented / Empathic

Human-Centred Design (HCD)

Grounded in community-oriented problem formulation, ethnographic photo-voice, participatory mapping, and vernacular coping mechanisms (Brown, 2008; IDEO, 2015). Prioritizes immediate human dignity, tenure, and daily survival routines.

Agency Role: Vulnerable citizens act as active problem definers and co-designers of vernacular adaptations (Corburn, 2005).
Spatial Blind Spot: Spatial planning and engineering authorities discard these community insights as purely anecdotal, legally ungrounded, or incompatible with municipal hydraulic safety regulations.
Failure Mode: Interventions remain trapped at the micro-scale (pilot community gardens or temporary sandbags), failing to alter statutory spatial masterplans or secure municipal capital budgets.
Literature: Corburn (2005) • Brown (2008) • Wamsler (2017)

Methodological Architecture • Operational Multi-Scalar Co-Production Protocol (MSCP)

The Methodological Approach: How Lived Evidence Reshapes Spatial Masterplans

The core innovation of this approach lies in resolving the scalar and epistemic mismatch of climate adaptation. Traditional planning operates at the macro-scale (1:50,000), modeling coastlines as uniform hazard lines while blind to localized livelihood flows. Conversely, human-centred design captures rich human realities at the street level (1:50), but lacks statutory authority and hydrodynamic engineering calibration. This research deploys a synchronized, four-tier multi-scalar protocol that translates qualitative lived experiences into binding statutory planning parameters:

PHASE 1 • MACRO Scale 1:50,000

Regional Hazard & Geodetic Calibration

Coupled hydrodynamic simulations (Delft3D Flexible Mesh) forced with IPCC AR6 SLR projections (SSP2-4.5 / SSP5-8.5) and Multi-temporal Persistent Scatterer InSAR (Sentinel-1A & ALOS PALSAR) calibrated with continuous GNSS CORS networks measuring vertical subsidence velocity ($V_v$ mm/yr to cm/yr).

Data Inputs: Satellite SAR, DEM, tide gauges, masterplans.
Method: PS-InSAR phase unwrapping + Delft3D-FM 2D flow.
Output: Regional hazard corridors & Disconnect Audit.
Tools: Delft3D-FM • SNAP Arup CRI (2015)
PHASE 2 • MESO Scale 1:5,000

Socio-Spatial Friction Counter-Mapping

Centimeter-accuracy UAV drone photogrammetry (2.5 cm/pixel orthomosaics) coupled with Participatory GIS (PGIS) transects. Field counter-mapping customary tenure boundaries, artisanal boat moorings, un-gazetted fish-drying yards, and informal alley drainage networks against official zoning lines.

Data Inputs: UAV imagery, cadastral parcels, GPS transects.
Method: Spatial Friction Index (SFI) overlay mapping.
Output: Socio-spatial friction maps & friction node registry.
Tools: QGIS • WebODM Corburn (2005)
PHASE 3 • MICRO Scale 1:50

Boundary Object Co-Design & Simulation

On-site HCD charrettes co-drafting scaled architectural-hydrodynamic vector cross-sections ("Boundary Objects"). Integrating physical engineering metrics (HAT tide datum, wave runup, slope stability) with lived human parameters (boat haulage winch slope, drying platform heights, doorstep barriers). Calibrated with EPA-SWMM.

Data Inputs: Photo-voice, time-use logs, water gauges, CAD.
Method: Boundary object vector sectioning + SWMM runoff.
Output: Scaled 1:50 architectural & nature-based prototypes.
Tools: AutoCAD • SWMM 5.2 Star & Griesemer (1989)
PHASE 4 • STATUTORY Scale 1:1

DAPP Policy Translation & Bylaws

Constructing Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP) with Adaptation Tipping Points (ATPs) anchored to lived vulnerability thresholds (e.g., drying yard flood duration, alley ponding depth). Translating prototypes into binding municipal zoning bylaws (Indonesian RTRW / Dutch Omgevingsplan) via Learning Alliances.

Data Inputs: Boundary objects, planning calendars, APBD budgets.
Method: DAPP decision trees + Learning Alliances (LAAs).
Output: Statutory masterplan amendments & budget lines.
Tools: DAPP Workbench Haasnoot (2013)

Scientific Rigour

Epistemic Triangulation & Cross-Scalar Calibration Protocol

A frequent criticism of participatory methods in spatial planning is that they yield anecdotal, non-quantified desires that hydraulic engineers cannot build. Conversely, pure engineering models often create mal-adaptive infrastructure because they cannot see informal micro-realities. The MSCP protocol bridges this divide through explicit two-way triangulation:

1. Top-Down Calibration

Satellite InSAR subsidence rates and Delft3D-FM tidal peaks set unyielding physical boundary conditions (e.g., minimum crest heights, wave runup safety limits) for community designs.

2. Bottom-Up Ground-Truthing

Community photo-voice and PGIS reveal micro-topographic flood pathways, customary boat draft requirements, and alley drainage clogs invisible to 5m DEMs.

3. Boundary Object Synthesis

The 1:50 scaled vector section serves as the formal boundary object where engineers and residents negotiate millimeter-level trade-offs, validated through EPA-SWMM simulation before codification.

Methodological Rigour • Peer-Reviewed Sourcing & Analytical Protocols

Protocols for Counting, Analyzing & Predicting in Climate Adaptation

This methodology integrates international quantitative resilience indices, boundary-object social theory, and hydrodynamic simulation models. Every calculation, spatial indicator, and scenario prediction is anchored in authoritative peer-reviewed literature and statutory standards:

Pillar I • Quantification 📐

1. Protocols for COUNTING

How indicators, lived vulnerabilities, and physical deformation velocities are measured and indexed into empirical baselines.

Arup CRI & ISO 37122 Global Frameworks: Standardized 156 city resilience metrics across 4 domains (Health & Wellbeing, Economy & Society, Infrastructure & Environment, Leadership & Strategy) [Arup & Rockefeller, 2015; ISO, 2019].
DROP & Social Vulnerability Index: Disaster Resilience of Place (DROP) model and MOVE framework quantifying place-based vulnerability differences between formal commercial and informal residential assets [Cutter et al., 2008; Birkmann et al., 2013].
PS-InSAR Radar Subsidence Velocity: Persistent Scatterer Synthetic Aperture Radar (Sentinel-1A & ALOS PALSAR) calibrated with continuous GNSS CORS baselines measuring mm/yr to cm/yr deformation [Chaussard et al., 2013; Andreas et al., 2019].
Household Livelihood Shock Sampling: Stratified micro-surveys (N=184) calculating annual working days lost, fish drying yard area inundated, and asset repair costs [University of Hull & Living with Water, 2020].
Standards: ISO 37122:2019 • InSAR Sentinel-1 • Arup CRI
Pillar II • Social Theory & Co-Production ⚖️

2. Protocols for ANALYZING

Theoretical dialectics used to deconstruct epistemic power imbalances and bridge the gap between municipal engineers and marginalized citizens.

Participatory Justice & Non-Tokenism: Few, Brown, & Tompkins (2007) and Arnstein (1969) deconstructing the "illusion of inclusion", moving adaptation from manipulative public hearings to delegated citizen co-production.
Urban Climate Justice & Equity Framework: Shi, Chu, Anguelovski, et al. (2016) and Pelling (2011) analyzing how top-down infrastructure perpetuates spatial inequalities, demanding procedural and recognitional justice for vulnerable communities.
Visual-Spatial Boundary Objects: Star & Griesemer (1989) theory: architectural cross-sections act as robust plastic artifacts enabling collaboration between engineers and informal residents without requiring epistemic consensus.
Citizen Co-Production in Spatial Planning: Wamsler (2017) and Albrechts (2013) demonstrating how co-production bridges ecosystem-based adaptation and institutional masterplans, securing statutory municipal legitimacy.
Street Science & Epistemic Translation: Corburn (2005) establishing protocols to combine localized experiential inquiry with professional environmental modeling into co-produced spatial science.
Theory: Few et al. (2007) • Shi et al. (2016) • Wamsler (2017) • Star & Griesemer (1989)
Pillar III • Hydrodynamic Scenarios 🌊

3. Protocols for PREDICTING

Computational hydrodynamic models and climate scenarios utilized to forecast future inundation extents and tipping points.

IPCC AR6 Sea Level Rise Scenarios: Downscaled Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5) projecting +0.38m to +0.85m sea level rise by 2050–2100 [IPCC, 2021/2022].
Delft3D-FM Coastal Hydrodynamics: Deltares (2021) flexible mesh shallow-water hydrodynamic simulation predicting storm surge propagation and wave dissipation across intertidal mangrove belts (-65% force).
EPA-SWMM 5.2 Compound Backwater: Hydrodynamic modeling of joint probability: high spring tide locking seawall flapped outfalls during 1:50 yr cloudburst storms, predicting trapped internal alley ponding.
Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP): Haasnoot et al. (2013) and van Herk et al. (2014) formulating adaptation tipping points (ATPs) and institutional Learning and Action Alliances.
Models: Delft3D-FM • EPA-SWMM 5.2 • DAPP • IPCC AR6

Theoretical & Methodological Framework

Project Aim & Methodological Framework

Reni Carica Ratriyani | Spatial Resilience Portfolio Submission

Mandate

1. The Primary Research Aim

To bridge the fundamental methodological divide between top-down, expert-driven adaptive planning and bottom-up human-centred design by establishing an evidence-based, multi-scalar spatial and visual methodology that operationalizes the agency of marginalized communities in statutory climate adaptation decisions across matching secondary coastal cities in the Global South and Global North.

Context & Empirical Problem: Adaptation studies recognize the intertwined complexities of built environments and societal vulnerabilities, yet methodological frameworks for harnessing the agency of marginalized communities remain generic. Top-down planning models treat communities as passive recipients, risking maladaptation and livelihood severance (South) or unembanked exclusion and climate gentrification (North). Conversely, human-centred design captures rich vernacular survival knowledge, but spatial planning authorities fail to integrate these insights into statutory zoning or capital budgets.

2. Four Strategic Operational Goals (G1–G4)

Goal 1 (Diagnostic / Empirical): Socio-Spatial Disconnect Audit Scale 1:50,000

Quantify and spatially audit the disconnect between macro-engineering compliance and localized lived vulnerability, diagnosing how top-down seawalls cause livelihood severance (South) or unembanked social housing exclusion (North). Tools: Arup CRI (156 indicators), InSAR, and MOVE framework.

Goal 2 (Methodological Framework): Multi-Scalar Visual Boundary Object Protocol Scale 1:5,000 ➔ 1:50

Construct and validate a multi-scalar visual protocol using "Boundary Objects" (Star & Griesemer, 1989; Corburn, 2005) that translates informal survival routines, boat haulage slopes, and fish-drying spaces into legitimate spatial planning parameters. Tools: Drone PGIS & vector sectioning.

Goal 3 (Co-Design & Simulation): Nature-Based & Amphibious Spatial Prototypes Scale 1:50

Co-produce and hydrodynamically simulate context-responsive, nature-based and amphibious spatial adaptation alternatives (stepped living mangrove revetments, articulated boat ramps, drying decks, water-squares) that resolve spatial friction and safeguard tenure. Tools: Delft3D-FM wave dissipation (-65%) & EPA-SWMM 5.2.

Goal 4 (Statutory Institutionalization): Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP) Scale 1:1

Institutionalize community-defined adaptation thresholds into municipal spatial masterplans (Indonesian RTRW / Dutch Omgevingsvisie) and capital expenditure budgets using Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP; Haasnoot et al., 2013). Tools: DAPP Decision Trees & Learning Alliances.

3. The Dual-Paradigm Gap & Authoritative Bridging Literature

How peer-reviewed literature conceptualizes the divide and provides the theoretical bridges:

Top-Down Planning Dilemma:

Relies on expert-driven hydraulic models and centralized budgets. Communities are subjected to the "illusion of inclusion"—manipulative or tokenistic consultation without real decision power (Few et al., 2007; Arnstein, 1969). Produces hard dikes that sever boat moorings or induce climate gentrification (Anguelovski et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2016).

Bottom-Up HCD Dilemma:

Captures rich community-oriented problem formulation, photo-voice narratives, and tacit survival routines (Corburn, 2005; Brown, 2008). However, spatial planning authorities discard these insights as anecdotal, legally ungrounded, or uncalibrated against engineering codes (Wamsler, 2017).

The Multi-Scalar Bridging Mechanism:

Visual-Spatial Boundary Objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989) combined with citizen co-production (Wamsler, 2017; Albrechts, 2013) and Street Science (Corburn, 2005) translate lived realities into quantitative engineering cross-sections. Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (Haasnoot et al., 2013) then embed these co-designed prototypes into statutory municipal bylaws.

4. Theoretical & Conceptual Glossary

Visual-Spatial Boundary Object: An artifact (vector cross-section, interactive map) that inhabits intersecting social worlds, satisfying the informational needs of both municipal hydraulic engineers and informal residents (Star & Griesemer, 1989).
Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP): An adaptive planning method establishing explicit Adaptation Tipping Points (ATPs) where changing climate or lived conditions trigger alternative investment pathways (Haasnoot et al., 2013).
